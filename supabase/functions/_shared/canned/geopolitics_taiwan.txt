Taiwan tracks 47 PLA aircraft after U.S. arms sale notification

Taipei — Taiwan's Ministry of National Defense reported tracking 47 People's Liberation Army aircraft and 9 PLA Navy vessels operating around the island over a 24-hour window ending Friday morning, with 26 aircraft crossing the median line of the Taiwan Strait or entering Taiwan's southwestern air defense identification zone.

The activity coincided with the U.S. State Department's notification to Congress earlier in the week of a proposed $1.4 billion arms sale to Taiwan, including spare parts and support contracts for F-16 fleets. The notification did not include any new weapons systems.

China's Eastern Theater Command issued a statement Friday afternoon describing the activity as a "joint combat readiness patrol" and characterizing the U.S. arms sale as "a serious provocation that grossly interferes in China's internal affairs."

Of the 47 aircraft tracked, 26 crossed the median line — a higher proportion than the typical patrol pattern over the past six months. Ministry analysts noted that the operation included an unusually high number of J-16 strike fighters and one KJ-500 airborne early warning aircraft, suggesting a deliberately high-signature posture rather than a routine surveillance flight.

Taiwan's defense ministry deployed combat air patrols, naval vessels, and coastal missile systems "in response," the standard formula used for incursions of this scale. There was no live-fire activity reported on either side, and commercial shipping through the strait was unaffected.

The activity falls short of the larger exercises China conducted around Taiwan in April and August 2024, both of which featured live-fire components and temporary shipping advisories. It is closer in scale to the multiple smaller-scale "patrols" conducted following high-profile U.S. congressional visits.

Taiwan's presidential office issued a brief statement reaffirming its commitment to "maintaining peace and stability across the strait." The U.S. State Department, asked to comment, repeated standard language about its "rock-solid" commitment to Taiwan's defense and its expectation that "differences across the strait will be resolved by peaceful means."

Regional partners have been quiet. Japan's defense ministry issued a routine status report acknowledging the activity but did not characterize it. The Philippines' Department of National Defense did not comment. South Korea, which is hosting senior U.S. defense officials this week for separate alliance meetings, has not issued a statement.

The Taiwanese semiconductor industry, the most-watched economic exposure to cross-strait tension, reported no operational impact. TSMC's main manufacturing complex in Hsinchu and the company's newer Kaohsiung site continued normal operations through the patrol window.

A senior Taiwanese defense official, speaking on background, characterized the activity as "loud signaling, not preparation," but said the median-line crossing rate over the past 90 days had risen to a level the ministry was actively re-evaluating in its threat-assessment cadence.
